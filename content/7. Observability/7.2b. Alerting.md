## Nobody watches a dashboard at 03:00

The dashboard from the previous page is excellent and it is useless at three in the morning, because nobody is looking at it. Alerting is the part that decides which of the numbers on that screen is allowed to wake a person up — and getting the answer wrong in either direction is expensive. Alert on everything and the third page of the night is ignored on principle. Alert on nothing and you find out from a customer.

Watch the shipped rules make that decision. Stop the collector, on purpose, with a task that restores it even if you interrupt the drill:

```bash
CHAOS_HOLD_SECONDS=180 mise run chaos:collector
```

It prints this much and then holds, silent, for three minutes before restarting the container:

```text
[chaos:collector] $ ./scripts/chaos-drill.sh collector
 Container agentops-observability-otel-collector-1 Stopping
 Container agentops-observability-otel-collector-1 Stopped
otel-collector stopped for 180s; watch ObservabilityCollectorDown move pending -> firing
```

Predict what happens before you poll. Prometheus scrapes that container every 15 seconds and the rule waits a 2-minute `for:` window. How long until the alert fires — 15 seconds, 2 minutes, or something in between? Leave that terminal holding and open a second one:

```bash
for i in $(seq 1 20); do
  state=$(curl -s 'http://localhost:9090/api/v1/rules?type=alert' \
    | jq -r '.data.groups[].rules[] | select(.name=="ObservabilityCollectorDown") | .state')
  echo "$(date +%H:%M:%S) $state"
  [ "$state" = firing ] && break
  sleep 15
done
```

```text
22:29:00 pending
22:29:15 pending
22:29:30 pending
22:29:45 pending
22:30:00 pending
22:30:15 pending
22:30:30 pending
22:30:45 pending
22:31:01 firing
```

Two minutes of `pending` — one scrape to notice the target is down, then the whole `for:` window — and then `firing`. Alertmanager has it a moment later:

```bash
curl -s 'http://localhost:9093/api/v2/alerts' \
  | jq -r '.[] | "\(.labels.alertname) \(.labels.severity) \(.status.state)"'
```

```text
ObservabilityCollectorDown page active
```

The agent kept answering the entire time. That gap — between "the service is fine" and "you are blind" — is the incident, and it is why this rule pages instead of filing a ticket.

When the hold expires, the first terminal finishes what it started, and it would do the same if you had interrupted it with `Ctrl-C`:

```text
 Container agentops-observability-otel-collector-1 Starting
 Container agentops-observability-otel-collector-1 Started
restored otel-collector
```

A scrape or two later the alert resolves by itself. The traces, metrics, and logs for the outage window are gone permanently, because nothing backfills them.

**You just drove a real alert through its whole lifecycle — pending, firing, routed, resolved — against the same rule file Kubernetes loads.**

## Page on symptoms, ticket on everything else

The rule that just fired is one of two that page. The split is deliberate: **page** only on sustained, user-visible symptoms and on a dark telemetry pipeline; everything else is a `ticket`-severity signal reviewed during working hours.

An **error budget** is the share of requests a service level objective allows to fail — here, 1% of spans, which is a 99% span-success [SLO]({{< relref "/0. Overview/0.8. Glossary.md#slo" >}}). A **burn rate** is how fast you are spending that budget relative to the objective's window. The paging rule uses both, plus a floor:

```yaml
- alert: AgentErrorBudgetBurn
  expr: |-
    agentops:calls:error_ratio_rate5m > (14.4 * 0.010)
      and agentops:calls:error_ratio_rate1h > (14.4 * 0.010)
      and sum(increase(agentops_calls_total{status_code="STATUS_CODE_ERROR"}[5m])) >= 3
  for: 2m
```

A burn rate of `14.4` against a 30-day 1% budget means that, at the current error ratio, the whole month's budget is gone in about two days — fast enough to justify waking someone. Requiring the fast 5-minute _and_ the slow 1-hour window to agree stops a brief spike from paging, and the separate three-failure floor handles sparse lab traffic where one bad request is 100% of the sample. One flaky request cannot page; three sustained failures can.

`agentops:calls:error_ratio_rate5m` is a **recording rule** — a query Prometheus precomputes and stores as its own series, so the alert reads one cheap value instead of recomputing a ratio on every evaluation.

Six alerts ship, each grounded in a metric this stack verifiably exports:

| Alert                            | Severity | Metric                                             | Fires on                                                                 | Window | Respond                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | -------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| `AgentErrorBudgetBurn`           | page     | `agentops_calls_total`                             | multiwindow burn plus ≥3 failures in 5m                                  | 2m     | [error budget](#the-four-responses-worth-memorising)       |
| `ObservabilityCollectorDown`     | page     | `up{job="otel-collector"}`                         | `up{job="otel-collector"} == 0` — traces, metrics, and logs are all dark | 2m     | [collector down](#the-four-responses-worth-memorising)     |
| `AgentTurnLatencyP95High`        | ticket   | `agentops_duration_seconds_bucket`                 | p95 across all agent spans above 15s over `[10m]`; tune to your hardware | 5m     | [latency](#the-four-responses-worth-memorising)            |
| `AgentTokenTelemetryMissing`     | ticket   | `agentops_tokens_token_total`                      | spans flowing but no token counter increase                              | 10m    | [collector down](#the-four-responses-worth-memorising)     |
| `AgentInjectionNeutralizedSpike` | ticket   | `agentops_guardrails_injections_neutralized_total` | more than 3 neutralized injections in 15m                                | 2m     | [guardrail / schema](#the-four-responses-worth-memorising) |
| `AgentTriageSchemaFailures`      | ticket   | `agentops_triage_report_schema_failures_total`     | any triage-schema failure in 15m                                         | 2m     | [guardrail / schema](#the-four-responses-worth-memorising) |

Two things to read carefully in that table. The collector's Prometheus exporter appends the unit suffix, which is why the token counter reads `agentops_tokens_token_total` rather than `agentops_tokens_total`. And Window is the rule's `for:` — how long the condition has to keep holding before `pending` becomes `firing` — which is a different number from the rate window inside the expression. `AgentTurnLatencyP95High` computes its quantile over `[10m]` of history and then insists the result stay above 15s for `5m`.

The host Compose stack loads these rules into Prometheus at `http://localhost:9090/alerts` and routes what fires to Alertmanager at `http://localhost:9093`, whose webhook points at a documented placeholder — replace that URL to integrate a real channel. The local Kubernetes overlay runs not merely equivalent rules but the same bytes: `infra/observability/prometheus-rules.yml` is a symlink to `infra/k8s/overlays/local/prometheus-rules.yaml`, which is the ConfigMap source kustomize reads. One file, two planes, and no way for them to drift apart — plus one consequence to keep in mind before you edit it, since a change you make "for Compose" is also a change to what the cluster loads. That overlay's Alertmanager deliberately has no integration at all: default-deny egress keeps notifications cluster-internal, and network policies allow only Prometheus to reach it. There is no external paging service anywhere in this course.

Two failure classes never page you, because they are judgements rather than thresholds: a **quality** incident, where answers are wrong rather than absent, and a **cost** incident, where a prompt change quietly doubled tokens. Treat both as first-class incidents anyway — [7.7. Incident Response]({{< relref "/7. Observability/7.7. Incident Response.md" >}}) shows how they surface.

{{% collapsible note "Deeper: why your first deliberate guardrail event may not fire its alert" %}}

`increase()` needs at least two samples of a series inside its window, so the very first guardrail or schema event after startup may register as nothing at all. Send a few before concluding the rule is broken. The same subtlety is why `AgentTokenTelemetryMissing` gates on real span traffic instead of using a bare `absent()`, which would fire on every idle lab. {{% /collapsible %}}

## The four responses worth memorising

An alert without a response is a notification. Each of the six maps onto one of four walks, and every walk starts from a signal you already know how to read.

**`AgentErrorBudgetBurn` — turns are failing.** The dashboard's error-ratio panel rises with the alert. Check the provider first: `curl -fsS http://127.0.0.1:4000/v1/models` through the gateway, and `ollama ps` on the host; in Kubernetes, `kubectl -n agentops get pods` and the agentgateway logs. The usual cause is a stopped Ollama, a missing `qwen3:4b-instruct` pull, a wrong `OLLAMA_HOST` binding, or a gateway route rejecting the upstream. Fix it, then watch the ratio decay and the alert resolve in Alertmanager rather than assuming it did.

**`AgentTurnLatencyP95High` — turns complete, slowly.** Open the slowest recent trace in Grafana's Tempo view and read which span dominates, exactly as in [7.1. Tracing]({{< relref "/7. Observability/7.1. Tracing.md" >}}); check `ollama ps` for model load state and the host for CPU pressure. Model cold starts, contention with other workloads, and oversized contexts from long sessions are the three common answers. If your hardware is simply slower, raise the threshold in the rules file deliberately — do not delete the alert.

**`ObservabilityCollectorDown` or `AgentTokenTelemetryMissing` — the dashboards flatten while the agent still answers.** The pipeline is broken, not the agent. Check the container with `docker compose -f infra/observability/compose.yaml ps otel-collector`, then ask whether the counter is being exported at all. Compose keeps `:8889` on the internal network, so ask the scraper that already reaches it:

```bash
docker compose -f infra/observability/compose.yaml exec -T prometheus \
  wget -qO- http://otel-collector:8889/metrics | grep agentops_tokens
```

Empty output is the answer, not a broken command: it means the counter is genuinely absent, which is the exact condition `AgentTokenTelemetryMissing` fires on. In Kubernetes, `kubectl -n agentops port-forward svc/otel-collector 8889:8889` first and curl the forwarded port. The usual causes are a collector crash or memory-limit kill, a port conflict from running host Compose and a forwarded in-cluster stack together, or an agent started without `OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT` so spans arrive from one process and metrics from none.

**`AgentInjectionNeutralizedSpike` or `AgentTriageSchemaFailures` — user traffic looks normal.** Filter Loki for recent turns with `{service_name="agentops-agent"}`, follow a matching line's `TraceID` link into Tempo, and inspect which tool output carried injection markers or which report failed validation. Injections usually mean adversarial content in the data the tools read — or someone running the offline red-team suite. Schema failures usually mean model or prompt drift after a swap: re-run the offline tests and evaluation gates and restore the pinned model and prompt combination before trusting new reports.

## Your turn: add an alert rule and its runbook

The chapter checkpoint asks for this one. A rule that has never fired is a hypothesis; a rule with a deterministic test is a check.

Predict first: `promtool test rules` replays a synthetic series through your expression without any Prometheus running. If your rule has a `for: 5m` window, what should the alert's state be at evaluation minute 3?

- **Mode**: `keep`.
- **Goal**: add one Prometheus alert tied to an observable outcome, drive it inactive-then-pending-then-firing against a fixed input series, and write the runbook an on-call engineer would open when it fires.
- **Files to touch**: `infra/k8s/overlays/local/prometheus-rules.yaml` — reach it through either path, they are the same file — plus a new `infra/observability/tests/<alert-name>.yml` rule test and a new `infra/observability/runbooks/<alert-name>.md` operator runbook.
- **Preflight**: choose a name, require both new paths to be absent with `test ! -e`, and require `git diff --quiet -- infra/k8s/overlays/local/prometheus-rules.yaml`. Check the real path, not the symlink: `git diff` on `infra/observability/prometheus-rules.yml` compares the link target string and stays quiet no matter what you write into the rules.
- **Steps**: model the rule and its test on the shipped `ObservabilityCollectorDown` pair — the existing test in `infra/observability/tests` is a complete worked example, including the exact annotation strings it asserts. Sustained tool-error rate and a p95 latency breach are both defensible choices.
- **Gate that proves completion**: both commands below pass, and your rule test asserts the alert inactive, pending for its declared window, firing with the exact runbook annotation, and then resolved.
- **Final state**: keep the rule, the deterministic test, and the runbook; `git status --short` shows no generated Prometheus data. Driving the condition against the live stack is optional runtime evidence and must be stopped afterwards.

```bash
promtool check rules infra/observability/prometheus-rules.yml
promtool test rules infra/observability/tests/<alert-name>.yml
```

```text
Checking infra/observability/prometheus-rules.yml
  SUCCESS: 8 rules found

  SUCCESS
```

That is both commands run back to back against the shipped rules only — two recording rules and six alerts. Your addition makes it nine.

Keep that operator runbook outside `agents/data/runbooks/`. That directory is the fictional service's immutable retrieval corpus, and dropping the agent platform's own on-call instructions into it would silently cross a trust and domain boundary: the agent would start retrieving and quoting its own operating manual as if it were customer evidence.

## What you can do now

- You drove `ObservabilityCollectorDown` from pending to firing to resolved and saw it reach Alertmanager, without editing a rule.
- You can explain why only two of the six alerts page, and what the `14.4` multiplier and the three-failure floor each prevent.
- For any of the six, you can name the first signal to open and the most likely cause.
- Your own rule passes `promtool check rules` and a `promtool test rules` case that asserts the pending-to-firing transition.

"We have monitoring" used to mean a dashboard existed somewhere. For you it now means something narrower and far more useful: a named rule, with a stated budget and a written response, decides whether your phone rings — and you can watch that rule work before it ever has to.
